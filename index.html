Jacquelyn Garcia — jag053@ucsd.edu

Proposition: Investing in intellectual property development indicates a country's innovation capacity and economic growth.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Focusing on countries with high IP receipts.
      • Score: 1 - These countries are more commonly recognized as having innovative economies.
      Rationale: By showing consistent high IP receipts in these nations over time, the visualization shows that there is a strong connection between economic success and IP activity.
  • Sorting the countries by their average IP receipts over the entire time period and displaying only the top few most consistent high performers.
      • Score: 0 - Sorting is standard, but I chose to focus only on the very top to emphasize my point.
      Rationale: Highlighting the success of a select few countries with high IP receipts over years can help strengthen the argument that investing in IP leads to sustained economic benefits.
  • Using a very saturated and bright yellow for the highest IP receipt values.
      • Score: 1 - Brighter colors can exaggerate the differences.
      Rationale: Making the highest values stand out can allow the viewer to focus on countries with economic success and overlook the performance of the other countries.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Selecting countries with high trademark activity but low IP receipts.
      • Score: 1 - The data shows that these countries have large amounts of trademark activity.
      Rationale: By showing that countries with high amounts of trademark activity but lower IP receipts, the visualization challenges the connecting between IP activity and economic benefit.
  • Using a color scale that emphasizes the volume of trademark applications.
      • Score: 0 - Accurately representing the count.
      Rationale: This highlights that high levels of activity does not equate to high value or economic impact from IP, going against the proposition.
  • Including a wider range of countries with high trademark activity, even if their IP receipts weren't as high.
      • Score: 1 - Shows a broader picture of trademark activity.
      Rationale: By showing that significant trademark activity also occurs in countries that don't necessarily have correspondingly high IP receipts, I suggest that investing in intellectual property doesn't automatically translate to high financial returns from IP.

Final Reflection

Creating two opposing visualizations for the same proposition was incredibly insightful. For the "for" argument, it seemed pretty straightforward to select and emphasize data points that would showcase the idea that high IP receipts are correlated with economic success. By focusing on the highest performing countries and their high values, the case seemed closed and shut. One of the first difficulties that I encountered began when I started to develop my "against" argument. I needed to leverage the trademark data in a way that high IP activity without high IP revenue could persusasively counter the initial proposition and insinuate that there was a lack of value in trademark activity. The easiest way that I could think of doing it was by selecting countries that emphasized volume instead of direct economic return.

One of the things that surprised me the most was how powerful framing is. Even though most of my design choices were mostly earnest like sorting or selecting specific data subsets, each of the resulting visualizations could guide the viewer's interpretation. Choosing which countries to include or exclude, which color scales were applied, and which countries heavily influenced the narrative that each visualization conveyed were fundamental for the design choices. This showed how the same underlying data can be used to support opposite conclusions simply by presenting the ideas through a different lense. It emphasizes that visualizations are very rarely neutral because most of the choices that are done are to implicitly sway perception.

This project has helped to shape my understanding of ethical analyses and visualizations. It is not just about accurate data representation but also about having critical awareness of the impact of your design choices. Ethical visualizations involve careful consideration about how things can be misinterprated. It is essential to draw a clear boundary between acceptable persuasion and clearly misleading viewers. It is a lot easier to spot intentionally distorted data or manipulating results but not as easy to pinpoint selective framing. The point is to allow freedom in design choices without misinterprating the underlying data, even if it does not portray the interpretation we initially had in mind.
